    How do Earth's shape and its motions around the Sun and on its axis of rotation influence our daily lives (for instance, the length of hours, the duration of months, seasons, and the calendar)?

     HINT: Consider how Earth's axial tilt and orbital position relative to the Sun change throughout the year, and how this affects the angle and duration of sunlight at your location.

     4.

    If a pinhead representing the Sun's position were placed at the center of Times Square in New York City, how many times would you have to walk around Earth's equator to reach it?

    HINT: Compare the Earth's distance from Sun to the Earth's equatorial circumference (i.e. perimeter).

    Earth's (equatorial) circumference = ~ 40,075 km

    Our distance to the Sun is ~149.6 million km, which is roughly 3,733 times Earth's equatorial circumference.
  • 5.

    How long does it take for a photon of light created at the center of the Sun through thermonuclear reactions to reach Earth?

    HINT: Consider what happens to photons as they travel through the Sun's dense interior before they leave the Sun's surface. Do they travel in a straight line at light speed the entire way? 6.

    What type of galaxy is the Milky Way, and what makes this type special? What are the other types of galaxies?

     HINT: Think about the structure you observe when you look at the Milky Way in the night sky. Is it a flat disk, a sphere, or something else? Research the Hubble classification scheme for different galaxy morphologies.
  • 7.

    Water molecules are found throughout our solar system. Where in our solar system is water found? What's so special about the water molecules found on Earth?

     HINT: Consider water in all its forms: solid ice, liquid water, and water vapor. Look at moons, planets, and even comets. Think about why having water in all three states of matter, and in such abundance, is important for life as we know it. 8.

    Why is the sky blue? Would the sky still be blue if Earth's atmospheric composition were different? Could we determine an exoplanet's atmospheric composition by observing its sky color from space?

     HINT: Think about Rayleigh scattering and how it depends on the wavelength of light and the size of molecules in the atmosphere. Then consider how different atmospheric gases and aerosols would scatter light differently.
  • 9.

    Do planets similar to Earth exist outside our solar system? How do we detect these planets and their atmospheres if we cannot see them directly?

     HINT: Think about exoplanet detection methods such as the transit method, radial velocity method, and direct imaging. For atmospheres, research transmission spectroscopy and what it reveals about atmospheric composition. 10.

    As an astrophysicist, you spend most of your time studying the properties of light from stars. But is there any other way to study the universe that does not rely on visible light?

    HINT: Think beyond the electromagnetic spectrum. Consider gravitational waves, cosmic rays, neutrinos, and other forms of radiation (infrared, ultraviolet, X-ray, radio). What does each of these tell us about distant objects?
  • 11.

    What are dark matter and dark energy? What observational evidence do we have for their existence?

     HINT: Consider galaxy rotation curves, gravitational lensing, and the accelerating expansion of the universe. What observations led astronomers to infer the existence of invisible matter and energy? 12.

    Do black holes contain dark matter? Separately, what observational evidence do astronomers use to support the existence of black holes? (Advanced Cosmology question)

     HINT: For the first part, consider whether dark matter can cross an event horizon. Then ask what an observer outside can measure about a settled black hole, and whether those measurements reveal what kind of matter fell in. The no-hair theorem may help you think this through. For the second part, compare different kinds of evidence: stars orbiting Sagittarius A*, X-rays from systems where matter is falling toward a black hole, gravitational waves from black hole mergers, and the Event Horizon Telescope's images of emission around black hole shadows. What does each observation tell us?
